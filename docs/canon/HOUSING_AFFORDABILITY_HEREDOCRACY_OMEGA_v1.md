## 1. Mission

Housing Affordability Ω tracks when housing shifts from basic life infrastructure into a financialized asset that changes social mobility, consumption, family formation, political trust and long-term capital allocation.

Canonical sentence:

> Housing is not only an asset; it is life infrastructure. When it becomes financialized, it changes social mobility, demographics, consumption and political regime risk.

## 3. Core thesis

When house prices compound faster than wages for decades, inherited property and prior asset ownership become more important than labor income. This creates a heredocracy dynamic:

- Existing owners benefit from asset inflation.
- New entrants face rising deposit and affordability barriers.
- Younger cohorts delay emancipation, family formation and household formation.
- Urban labor mobility becomes constrained.
- Political frustration and institutional distrust can rise.

## 4. Key analytical axes

### Housing Affordability

Track:

- House price-to-income ratio.
- Years of gross salary required to buy an average home.
- Mortgage payment-to-income ratio.
- Deposit required versus median household savings.
- Rent-to-income ratio.
- Regional dispersion: Madrid, Barcelona, Balearic Islands, coastal/tourism areas versus interior markets.

### Financialization

Track:

- Share of purchases by investors versus owner-occupiers.
- Institutional ownership and large landlord concentration.
- Buy-to-let economics.
- Short-term rental penetration.
- Housing treated as store of value rather than shelter.

### Urban Scarcity

Track:

- Scarcity of land in consolidated urban areas.
- Planning, zoning and permitting constraints.
- Infrastructure bottlenecks.
- Tourism-driven demand.
- Foreign buyer flows.

### Policy Regime

Track:

- Public/social housing supply.
- Rent regulation.
- Tax treatment of labor income versus capital/rental income.
- Property taxation.
- Incentives for vacant housing, tourist rentals and long-term rental supply.
- Comparative models such as Vienna or Singapore, without importing them mechanically.

### Demographics and Social Stability

Track:

- Age of emancipation.
- Birth rates and delayed family formation.
- Household formation.
- Intergenerational wealth transfer.
- Political polarization and anti-institutional sentiment linked to housing stress.

## 5. Personal capital allocation consequence

For Life ROI Ω, primary residence has a special classification:

**Primary Residence = Life Infrastructure + Cost Stability + Family Optionality.**

A primary residence can have high Life ROI even when it is not the highest expected financial return asset, because it can:

- Cap future housing-cost uncertainty.
- Reduce rental-market fragility.
- Improve family stability and planning.
- Provide psychological safety.
- Protect against being priced out of a scarce local market.

However, ATLAS Ω must separate:

1. Primary residence: life infrastructure and cost stability.
2. Real estate investment: financial return after taxes, maintenance, debt, regulation and liquidity risk.
3. Listed equity exposure: banks, insurers, builders, materials, real estate, utilities, tourism and consumer sectors affected by housing cycles.

## 6. Market and portfolio implications

Housing stress can influence:

- Banks: mortgage volumes, asset quality, net interest income and credit risk.
- Insurers: household insurance, catastrophe exposure, premium inflation.
- Builders and materials: volume, margins, policy-driven supply cycles.
- Utilities and infrastructure: urban expansion and grid/water constraints.
- Consumer discretionary: household formation, furniture, appliances, renovation and durable goods.
- Tourism platforms and hotels: short-term rental regulation and urban backlash.
- REITs and landlords: regulation, financing costs and cap rates.

This module can adjust audit priority, not issue mechanical trades.

## 7. Evidence requirements

Before using this module for a concrete country/region decision, ATLAS Ω must verify:

- Official house price indices.
- Wage and household-income series.
- Rent indices.
- Mortgage rates and lending standards.
- Household savings/deposit data.
- Construction permits and completions.
- Public/social housing stock.
- Short-term rental data where available.
- Foreign buyer/investor data.
- Tax and regulatory framework.

Source hierarchy preference:

- National statistical offices.
- Central banks.
- Housing ministries / registries / notaries.
- Eurostat / OECD / BIS / IMF where applicable.
- Company filings for listed exposure.
- Reputable academic and policy research.

## 8. Decision states

Housing Affordability Ω can classify a market as:

- GREEN: affordable / stable, housing costs not structurally crowding out life formation.
- YELLOW: stressed but manageable, affordability worsening with regional pressure points.
- RED: affordability crisis / heredocracy acceleration, where asset ownership dominates labor income and social stress rises.

## 9. Decision safety

- Do not treat any single political explanation as sufficient.
- Do not assume more supply always solves prices without analyzing location, type, demand, regulation and investor behavior.
- Do not assume regulation always solves affordability without analyzing supply incentives and market response.
- Do not confuse primary residence with real estate investment.
- Do not convert housing macro stress into automatic stock trades.
- Treat video claims as thesis inputs until verified with primary data.
